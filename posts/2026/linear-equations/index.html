Solving Linear Equations

The geometry of elimination, and why it leads to LU

Linear algebra
Published

October 24, 2026

In the previous post, I looked at matrices through vectors and linear transformations. This time I look at them from another angle: systems of linear equations. What interested me most was the geometry behind solving these systems, and how Gaussian elimination leads naturally to the LU decomposition.

1 Lines, Planes and Their Normals

Consider two equations in two unknowns:

\[ \begin{cases} x-2y=1,\\ 3x+2y=11. \end{cases} \]

Each equation describes a line in the plane, and the solution is the point where the two lines cross, \((x,y)=(3,1)\) (Figure 1). In matrix form, the system is

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\3&2\end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}}_{\vec{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1\\11\end{bmatrix}}_{\vec{b}} \]

With three equations in three unknowns, each equation describes a plane in space, and the solution is the point the three planes share. For example,

\[ \begin{cases} x+y+z=4,\\ x-y+z=2,\\ 2x-z=0 \end{cases} \]

has the solution \((x,y,z)=(1,1,2)\) (Figure 2). The geometry changes from lines to planes, but the algebra is still \(A\vec{x}=\vec{b}\).

Whether the lines or planes meet in a single point depends on how they are tilted, and a plane’s tilt is described by its normal vector, which I remember from high-school geometry. The plane \(ax+by+cz=d\) has the normal vector \(\vec{n}=(a,b,c)\), assuming \((a,b,c)\ne(0,0,0)\). To see why, take any two points \(P_1=(x_1,y_1,z_1)\) and \(P_2=(x_2,y_2,z_2)\) on the plane. Both satisfy its equation, and subtracting the two equations gives, in the language of the dot product,

\[ (a,b,c)\cdot(x_1-x_2,\ y_1-y_2,\ z_1-z_2)=0 \]

The second vector runs along the plane. Since this holds for any two points, \((a,b,c)\) is perpendicular to every direction in the plane, which is exactly what a normal vector is. In the same way, the line \(ax+by=c\) has the normal \((a,b)\).

The normals tell us when a unique solution exists. In two dimensions, independent normals give two lines that are not parallel, so they cross at one point. Dependent normals give parallel or coincident lines, so there is either no solution or infinitely many. In three dimensions, three independent normals mean the planes meet at exactly one point. If the normals span fewer than three directions, a unique solution is impossible, and the system has no solution or infinitely many, depending on whether the equations are consistent. In matrix language, the number of independent normal vectors is the rank of \(A\).

2 What Elimination Does Geometrically

To solve a system, we usually eliminate variables until it becomes upper triangular. I found it more interesting to ask what each step means geometrically.

Go back to the two lines and replace the second equation with the second equation minus three times the first:

\[ (3x+2y)-3(x-2y)=11-3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 8y=8 \]

The new line, \(y=1\), is different from the original second line, but it still passes through \((3,1)\). The solution is unchanged because the step can be undone: adding three times the first equation back gives the original second equation.

The normal vectors change in the same way as the equations:

\[ \vec{n}_2'=\vec{n}_2-3\vec{n}_1=(3,2)-3(1,-2)=(0,8) \]

So subtracting a multiple of one equation from another turns the second line around the fixed intersection point (Figure 3). Elimination picks the multiple that makes \(\vec{n}_2'\) point straight up: the line becomes horizontal, \(x\) disappears from its equation, and we can read off \(y=1\).

The same idea applies in three dimensions. Replacing one plane’s equation by a combination of two equations changes that plane, but keeps the line where the two planes meet. Elimination keeps turning planes like this (Figure 4) until the system is upper triangular: the last plane is horizontal, the one above it has no \(x\) in its equation, and we can read off \(z\), then \(y\), then \(x\).

3 Recording Elimination: \(PA=LU\)

Gaussian elimination turns \(A\) into an upper triangular matrix \(U\). Instead of treating it as a temporary calculation, we can record the operations that produced \(U\). In the two-line example, subtracting three times the first row from the second is itself a matrix:

\[ E=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-3&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad EA=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\0&8\end{bmatrix}=U \]

Undoing the step gives \(A=E^{-1}U\):

\[ A= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\3&1\end{bmatrix}}_{L} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\0&8\end{bmatrix}}_{U} \]

That is an LU decomposition. \(U\) is what elimination produces, and the lower triangular \(L\) stores the multiplier, \(3\), in a form that rebuilds \(A\).

With more than one step, we need one fact about inverses. For a square matrix \(A\), the inverse \(A^{-1}\), when it exists, satisfies \(A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I\), and the inverse of a product reverses the order:

\[ (AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1} \]

This makes sense if we think of matrices as transformations applied one after another: \(AB\) applies \(B\) first and then \(A\), so to undo it, we first undo \(A\), then undo \(B\).

The three planes above take three steps: subtract \(1\) times row 1 from row 2 (\(E_{21}\)), \(2\) times row 1 from row 3 (\(E_{31}\)), and then \(1\) times the new row 2 from row 3 (\(E_{32}\)):

\[ E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}A=U=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&-2&0\\0&0&-3\end{bmatrix} \]

Undoing the three steps in reverse order,

\[ A=E_{21}^{-1}E_{31}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}U= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\1&1&0\\2&1&1\end{bmatrix}}_{L}U \]

\(L\) holds the three multipliers, \(1\), \(2\) and \(1\), each in the position of the entry it eliminated. The reversed order is what keeps them clean. The forward product \(E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}\) has \(-1\) in its bottom-left corner, not \(-2\): it changes row 3 using row 2 after row 2 has already been changed, so the multipliers mix. Multiplied in the reverse order, the inverses simply put each multiplier in its place.

Why keep \(L\)? Suppose we need to solve \(A\vec{x}=\vec{b}\) for many right-hand sides \(\vec{b}\) while \(A\) stays the same. With \(A=LU\), each new \(\vec{b}\) needs only two triangular systems,

\[ L\vec{y}=\vec{b},\qquad U\vec{x}=\vec{y} \]

solved by forward and back substitution. We could also save the row operations and replay them on each new \(\vec{b}\). LU is a structured way to do exactly that. The saving is large: eliminating an \(n\times n\) matrix takes about \(n^3/3\) multiplications, while the two triangular solves take about \(n^2\).

One complication remains: not every invertible matrix can be factored this way without row exchanges. For example,

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix} \]

is invertible, but its first pivot is zero, so the rows must be exchanged first. A permutation matrix \(P\) records the exchanges, which gives the general form

\[ PA=LU \]

To solve \(A\vec{x}=\vec{b}\) with it, we permute the right-hand side the same way: \(LU\vec{x}=P\vec{b}\).

For me, the main idea is that LU is not a separate trick from Gaussian elimination. It is the work elimination already does, written down so that it can be reused.

4 Transpose, for Now

The transpose \(A^T\) exchanges the rows and columns of \(A\); I picture its entries flipped across the main diagonal. A matrix with \(A=A^T\) is called symmetric. Like the inverse, the transpose of a product reverses the order, \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\). I can verify this from the definitions, but the connection between the transpose, dot products and orthogonality will be more interesting later, so I’ll leave it here.1

1 Update (2026-10-29): The connection showed up in the next post. A dot product can be written with the transpose, \(\vec{x}\cdot\vec{y}=\vec{x}^T\vec{y}\), so \(A^T\vec{e}\) collects the dot products of \(\vec{e}\) with every column of \(A\) at once. Least squares needs the error to be perpendicular to every column, \(A^T(\vec{y}-A\hat{\vec{w}})=\vec{0}\), and that gives the normal equations. See Vector Spaces and Least Squares.

For now, the two ideas I want to keep from this post are the geometric meaning of elimination and the way LU records it.
